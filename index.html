        Your research under this graph: what the two papers say

        Paper 1: per-kiln, per-date firing detection (remote sensing physics).

        • Sentinel-2 SWIR cannot see a kiln fire: an insulated roof at 70–90 °C adds only about 0.14% to the pixel signal, below sensor noise. Satellite mode "SWIR heat only" is therefore close to random here.
        • Fire activity must be inferred from several signals: coal and brick piles and smoke plumes (Sentinel-2), ground heat after daytime (Landsat/ECOSTRESS), and radar yard changes (Sentinel-1) that work through fog.
        • Records are noisy: the registry says every kiln is zigzag but only 14% are; some kilns fire at night; fire-alert points mostly show crop burning, not kilns.

        Paper 2: kiln technology recognition across the India–Pakistan border (literature review and roadmap).

        • Finding the kiln transfers across the border (CA mAP50 drops only 1.6 points) but naming its technology fails: 56% of Pakistani FCBK kilns are called zigzag, against 17% in India.
        • Balanced sampling fixes class separation but over-reports zigzag (37% instead of 14%); prior calibration is the proposed fix.
        • Wrong technology means wrong smoke totals: zigzag kilns emit over 60% less. The model shows this as "smoke inventory error".

        Updates and recommendations from the papers. Drop SWIR thresholds; combine optical, thermal and radar; freeze a kiln registry (SentinelKilnDB 12,177 + Hamdani about 11,000 + EPA 7,891 IDs) as the spatial anchor; train a student on public sensors from daily 3 m images; correct label shift (class probability matching, logit adjustment); and dispatch only confident calls (selective prediction). Full list and the link to each model setting: Research tab, section 13.

                    Agentic AI team · v17

                    A team of twelve agents runs the city's decision desk. Seven specialist officers (health, air quality, traffic, utilities, finance, welfare and the birth centre) watch their areas every simulated minute, find the cause of each problem and rank several options, including out-of-the-box ones: air purifiers, building filters, cloud seeding, water-mist guns, free buses, odd-even cars, cash relief, vaccination drives and more. A strategist joins measures into packages; a coordinator merges the plans; a safety reviewer checks every option (including its price against the treasury) before you see it; a forecaster tries the top options on a copy of the city as it is right now; and an auditor records every decision.

                    Strict consent. Nothing in the city changes unless you press Approve. Big decisions, standing rules, hard-to-undo steps and anything the safety reviewer flags need a second click on Confirm. Every proposal shows its price and an exit rule. If you tick the box above the list when you approve, the measure may ease in steps and lift by itself when things get better (never the hard-to-undo ones); otherwise lifting is a proposal you decide on. Deny sends an option to the deny list for the days you choose (it returns sooner only if its problem becomes an emergency, and then still only as a question). Never keeps it away completely until you allow it.

                    Can this be published? An honest answer

                    What it can do. It produces a complete, reproducible draft: introduction, method description (ODD-style), the agent's equations, experiment design, results tables with confidence intervals, limitations, an AI-assistance statement and a reference list whose entries were checked against the publisher or archive pages. Anyone with the repository can rerun the benchmark and get the same numbers.

                    What it cannot do. It cannot promise acceptance, and it cannot supply what reviewers will look for first:

                    • Validation against a real city. Right now the city is synthetic. Use the CSV loader on the AI Agent tab with real air-quality, temperature or case data, report how well the model matches (error, bias, correlation), and say plainly where it does not.
                    • Evidence that the agent is better than strong alternatives, not only than doing nothing. The benchmark already includes a fixed pack and the city's own autopilot; add more if a reviewer would expect them.
                    • Sensitivity analysis of the settings you chose yourself: the burden weight (0.4), each option's expected effect and burden. Report what changes when you vary them.
                    • Related work in depth. The generated reference list is short on purpose. Add the literature you have actually read, including your own brick-kiln papers.

                    Realistic routes (check each venue's current rules before you submit): put the code, data and paper on a repository that gives a DOI (for example Zenodo through GitHub); post a preprint (some servers need an endorsement or affiliation for first-time authors); ask your supervisor about a student or workshop track or a simulation-methods journal.                     Research notes

                    From a research specification to a running city

                    This tab records how the Master Build Specification: Smart City Digital Twin using Agent-Based Modelling was turned into Outbreak Lab v5, what each subsystem does, and, just as important, what was not built. Every claim below can be checked in the app: the Smart city tab shows the numbers and the validation checks.

                    1. The design in one paragraph

                    People (agents) live in a 1 km × 0.6 km city. Their routines (home, work, school, five daily prayers, shopping at the mart or the sabzi mandi, funerals) put them in shared places, where an infection can pass. The same city also has infrastructure: a power grid with solar and a battery, a water network, rubbish bins and trucks, buses, ambulances, fire engines, repair crews and sensors. The two layers are coupled, so a power cut does not stay a power cut: signals flash, crashes rise, pumps stop, water runs short, hygiene suffers, and homes get more infections.

                    power cut → signals flash & lights off → more crashes → more ambulances, more injured
                    power cut → pumps stop → water shortage → hygiene stress → home infection × (1 + hygiene effect × shortage)
                    strike → bins overflow → burning (smoke) + blocked drains (flood) + sanitation effect → infection
                    sensor failure → the smog school-closure rule is wrong or blind → schools open in smog (or close for nothing)

                    3. The update cycle (what happens in one tick = 1 minute)

                    1. New day (at midnight): weather, random incidents for the day, sensor failures, funerals, flights and everyone’s plan for the day.
                    2. Weather, daylight, temperature and flooding.
                    3. People follow their plans: walk, cycle, wait at a stop, ride a bus, drive, or stay inside.
                    4. Traffic: accidents, ambulances (cars pull over), air ambulance, motorway, aircraft, buses, hearse, delivery vans, fire engines, waste trucks, crews.
                    5. Every 10 minutes: count who is where, then power, water, waste and sensors (in that order, because water pumps need power and waste needs the truck fleet).
                    6. Services: requests are matched to crews; fires burn and are fought.
                    7. Air pollution (including smoke from fires and burning rubbish).
                    8. Every 5 minutes: infection contacts in shared places and in buses.
                    9. Health states change; hospital admissions; deaths; funerals are queued.
                    10. Every hour: all history series are recorded (charts and CSV).

                    Changes to settings made while running are applied before the next tick. Where two agents compete for one thing (one bed, one crew, one road junction) the first in the list, or the nearest, wins; the rule is fixed so the same seed gives the same run.

                    4. Energy

                    demandclass = Σ places (base load × occupancy factor × heat factor × tariff elasticity × demand response)
                    supply = solar + grid import (≤ grid limit; 0 in an outage) + battery discharge (≤ 25% of capacity per hour)
                    solar = peak × sun elevation × cloud × dust × (1 − 0.004 × (T − 25))
                    battery: charge from spare solar, discharge in the evening peak; 92% efficiency each way; always between 0 and capacity
                    priority when short: critical (hospital, signals, pumps) > public > industry > homes > street lights

                    Policies: uniform cuts everybody by the same share; critical protects the top classes first; rolling cuts home feeders in turn. The hospital diesel generator starts when supply is short. Check: in = out every step (the residual is tracked, see the Smart city tab).

                    Assumptions and limits: loads are averages per building type, not appliances; one grid node; no voltage or frequency; the battery is one lumped unit; tariffs affect only demand, not supply.

                    5. Water

                    inflow = treatment inflow × (0.45 in drought) × power available + rain
                    pumped = min(demand ÷ (1 − leak) + bursts, pump capacity × power, tank ÷ dt + inflow)
                    tanknew = tank + (inflow − pumped) × dt − overflow
                    shortage = (demand − delivered) ÷ demand, smoothed

                    Demand follows occupancy, the time of day (morning and evening peaks), heat, conservation and price. Check: the change of the tank equals inflow minus pumped minus overflow. Limits: one tank, no pipe pressure or network topology, bursts are points that only add loss.

                    6. Waste

                    Every block has one bin. Waste is produced by homes (by who is inside), shops, the mandi, the hospital and factories, and scaled by the waste setting. Trucks collect the fullest bin or follow a fixed round (900 kg per load), unload at the depot, and a share is recycled. A bin over capacity overflows; some of the overflow is burnt (extra PM2.5), clogs the drains (flooding in rain) and raises infection risk at nearby homes.

                    bins at start + produced = collected + burnt + still in bins

                    7. Sensors and the digital twin

                    For each sensor, four numbers are kept apart: actual (the true value in the model), measured (actual + noise + calibration bias), received (measured, after the delay and after lost messages) and dashboard (what the control room shows: the average of fresh air readings). A failed sensor stops reporting and a repair request is opened. When “rules use sensor readings” is on, the smog school-closure rule uses the dashboard value, and the app counts decisions that differ from what the true air quality would have required.

                    8. Daily life added in v5

                    • Five daily prayers at 05:10, 12:45, 16:15, 19:15 and 20:30 (model times). Devout people walk to the mosque; attendance is a setting.
                    • Funerals: after a death the family and neighbours attend the janazah prayer (13:35) and the burial (14:30) the next day; a hearse drives to the graveyard.
                    • Shopping at the mart (from 09:00) or the sabzi mandi (05:30–13:00); a share of mart orders arrive by delivery van.
                    • Public transport: two bus routes with stops on both sides of the road, waiting queues, buses that obey signals, crowding, and infection on board. Cyclists ride 2.4 times as fast as walkers.
                    • Ambulance realism: cars pull over for a siren; the model counts how often.

                    9. Storms and lightning (v6)

                    Rain is drawn in three depth layers with splashes, wet roads, puddles and lamp reflections; heavy rain darkens the screen edges and plays a rain sound. In a thunderstorm (heavy rain above 0.82, or the “Thunderstorm all day” weather) the model throws lightning at the city at the chosen rate (strikes per hour). Each strike lands at a random point; with 12% chance it burns out the transformer of the nearest block (a power cut, and a repair request for the crews) and with 1.5% chance it starts a small fire. Lightning uses its own random stream, so a storm never changes the draws used elsewhere. After a rain on a bright day a rainbow appears. Distant sheet lightning and forked bolts are decoration; the bolts that hit the city are real events and are counted on the Smart city tab.

                    11. Wi-Fi, emergency alerts and the city scorecard (v7)

                    Wi-Fi. Up to ten public access points sit at the hospital, bazaar, mart, airport, mosque, university, government offices, a school, a park and an office tower. Each has a range, a capacity (Mbit/s) and a battery. People with a phone (about 90% of adults, 55% of children, 45% of elders, fixed by id) connect to the nearest access point in range. Demand follows the time of day; when demand is above capacity, link quality drops to capacity/demand. In a power cut an access point runs on its battery and then goes dark. Access points also break at random for a few hours (Access-point failures), or you can switch one off with the Incident tool.

                    Why it matters for the twin. A sensor in a Wi-Fi dead zone uses a weaker mobile link (95% of readings arrive); a sensor under a working access point loses fewer readings (85–100%, by link quality). So a Wi-Fi failure shows up as more missing readings and a wider gap between the true city and the control-room dashboard.

                    Emergency alert. The button sends a message to every phone. Everyone with a phone counts as reached; people who are out (not at home, not in a hospital) go home with probability alert compliance. It is logged in the audit trail and needs the Operator role.

                    Scorecard. Seven 0–100 scores, averaged: power reliability (share of demand served), water security (share of demand met), clean streets (share of bins not overflowing), connectivity (share of phones online weighted by link quality), air quality (100 − AQI/3.5), road & fire safety (100 − 3×accidents/day − 8×fires/day) and public health (falls with the share exposed or infectious and with deaths). These are simple teaching rules, not official indices.

                    Limits. Network traffic is aggregate (no packets or routing); phone ownership is a fixed hash; uncovered sensors use a flat mobile-link success rate. The research paper from your shared link could not be opened by me, so it has not been added yet. Paste its text or attach the file and I will add it here and align the model.

                    • Add real weather and air-quality data for one city and calibrate the model against it.
                    • Let kilns differ (size, fuel, technology) and add a licence or fine policy.
                    • Run 30 or more seeds per case and report ranges.
                    • Add crop-residue burning and a heating-fuel season in winter.

                    Recommendations

                    • Shut kilns for the winter smog months: it is the single biggest effect found here, and larger than anything else tested.
                    • Move kilns to clean zig-zag technology; it helps in every season, not only winter.
                    • Do not rely on one optical satellite alone: combine it with ground sensors and thermal imaging, because fog hides kilns.
                    • Use a smog rule that stops construction and closes schools when AQI is over 200, and spray water on building sites.
                    • Check the satellite regularly against the ground truth and publish its error rate.

                    13. Your two research papers, and how the model uses them (v9)

                    Download: Paper 1: Per-kiln, per-date firing activity detection (.docx) · Paper 2: Cross-border kiln technology recognition, literature review and roadmap (.docx). Both files are also in the papers/ folder of the GitHub zip.

                    Paper 1: Per-kiln per-date brick kiln firing activity detection from satellite remote sensing

                    • Problem. More than 100,000 kilns across South Asia cause 8–16% of regional fine particles (30–91% of winter pollution in some airsheds). Maps of where kilns are exist; knowing whether one particular kiln is firing today is the unsolved part.
                    • Physics result. Fixed-chimney and zigzag kilns hide an 800–950 °C fire under an ash roof at 70–90 °C, and the chimney is only about 3 m2. In a 20 m Sentinel-2 SWIR pixel the heat adds about 0.14% of the sunlit background, far below the sensor noise. Simple SWIR hot-spot thresholds cannot work.
                    • Proposed method. Four signals: (1) Sentinel-2 reflective clues (coal piles, fresh fired bricks, bare trench, smoke-plume pattern); (2) ground heat difference from Landsat 8/9 and ECOSTRESS thermal bands; (3) Sentinel-1 radar yard coherence, which works through fog; (4) daily 3 m PlanetScope images as a teacher for the public sensors. Then calibrate for label shift and send inspectors only for confident calls.
                    • Label noise in Punjab. The authorities reported all kilns as zigzag but the satellite benchmark finds only 14.2% (1,731 of 12,177) are; many "conversions" are an oval kiln with a fan bolted on. Fire-alert points from MODIS/VIIRS are almost all crop-stubble fires, not kilns. Some kilns bank fires by day and fire hard at night.
                    • Planned tests. Baseline 1: SWIR heat only (expected near random, AUC 0.52–0.58). Baseline 2: coarse tehsil-level trace gas (poor per kiln, F1 under 0.45). Model A optical (F1 about 0.78 in clear sky), Model B plus ground heat (catches night firing), Model C plus radar and calibration (works in fog). These are the paper's expected outcomes, not results yet.

                    Paper 2: Cross-border brick-kiln technology recognition: literature review, novelty audit, roadmap

                    • Finding. A detector trained on Indian 10 m Sentinel-2 images still finds kilns in Pakistan (class-agnostic mAP50 falls only 1.6 points), but naming the technology collapses: 56% of Pakistani FCBK kilns are called zigzag, against 17% in India.
                    • Adaptation trap. Random target samples give no gain because zigzag is rare in Pakistan (about 14% against 47% in India). Class-balanced samples improve separation but the model then reports 37% zigzag instead of 14%.
                    • Novelty verdict. The overall "location transfers, subtype does not" idea is known; the India–Pakistan label-shift audit and the adaptation catch-22 are new. The main risk is a pure diagnosis paper without a working fix.
                    • Ranked gaps. 1 background soil vs kiln footprint; 2 two-stage design (locator plus calibrated crop classifier); 3 how wrong technology counts distort emission inventories; 4 multispectral cues (NDBKI); 5 logit-adjusted losses; 6 super-resolution; 7 season and operating-phase confusion.
                    • Plan. Three stages: diagnose the shift; adapt actively and calibrate the prior; triage with a confidence gate to build the emission inventory. About 17.5 GPU hours on a T4; target a 6-page IEEE paper.

                    14. Resources, chronic illness and real-world scale (v11)

                    This version turns Outbreak Lab from an epidemic-only tool into a general city simulator: resources (water, electricity, fuel, fog/visibility) are now independently controllable, several non-epidemic illnesses run alongside the main disease, and a real-world scale panel estimates what a full-size city would need. The city also no longer stops automatically when the outbreak ends — "Never stop (real-world mode)" keeps every system running so the model can be used to study a normal city, not only an epidemic.

                    Resources & realism

                    • Water use per person and electricity use scale every home, office and public building's demand independently of each other, so low-flow-fixture or energy-efficiency policies can be tested without touching anything else.
                    • Fuel: petrol price and vehicle efficiency are set directly; the model tracks litres burned and PKR spent, hour by hour, from actual traffic in the simulation (driving agents and moving vehicles).
                    • Fog density is now a baseline haze independent of the rain/fog weather spells, combined with season and night-time. At its default (25%) it barely changes a clear day; at maximum, on a winter night, it produces dense fog on its own.
                    • Real city this represents: a multiplier (1–50,000×) scales the simulated 500-person city's water, electricity, fuel, waste, medicine and infrastructure needs up to a real population size, shown on the Smart city tab's "Real-world scale" card.

                    Chronic conditions & common illness

                    • Each adult or elder has a chance of diabetes, high blood pressure or asthma, set by sliders. These raise the chance of serious illness from the main epidemic disease by about 35%, 20% and 25% respectively, on top of age, vaccination, smoking and air quality.
                    • A separate, ongoing common illness system gives people flu, cough, fever or a sore throat — unrelated to the epidemic, rising with bad air and in winter, needing medicine but not causing death. A pharmacy stock is drawn down and resupplied daily; running low raises risk slightly for people with a chronic condition.

                    Honest limits

                    • The chronic-illness severity multipliers (35%, 20%, 25%) are reasonable assumptions, not fitted to a clinical study.
                    • Common illness uses one combined rate for flu, cough, fever and sore throat; it does not model transmission between people the way the main epidemic disease does.
                    • The real-world scale is a straight linear multiplication of the simulated city's resource use. A real city's infrastructure does not scale perfectly linearly (economies and diseconomies of scale exist), so treat the numbers as an order-of-magnitude estimate, not an engineering design figure.
                    • Fuel use is computed from simulated agents' and vehicles' speeds and counts, not from real traffic counts for any specific city.

                    17. The agentic AI mayor and loading your own city (v14)

                    What the agent is. A goal-driven planning agent. Every few simulated hours it observes six pressures (health, air, roads, power, water, connectivity, each 0 to 100%), picks the actions from a toolbox of 17 real city controls that best serve the chosen goal, acts or asks you first, and 12 hours later checks whether the targeted pressure did better than its pre-action trend. Measures that look harmful are lifted, and each action's track record (kept in this browser) raises or lowers how much the agent trusts it next time. Big decisions (closing schools, banning gatherings, ordering clean kilns) can require your approval, and "advise only" mode never acts.

                    Honest limits. It is rule-based, not a language model: it does not understand text and its urgency rules and thresholds are my design choices, not fitted values. The "did it help?" check is a heuristic against a trend line, and weather or the epidemic's own course can fool it; one action's lesson mixes in everything else that changed. The Test panel is the fairer check: it reruns the same city and seed with and without the agent. Even so it is one random seed, so small differences are noise. (In v14 the same seed could give slightly different results, because bus-stop queues were shared between simulations; v15 fixes this, so the same seed and settings now always give the same result.) "Search the best policy" tests each package one at a time from Monday 06:00 on a fresh city, not from the current state, so combinations and timing may behave differently.

                    Your city from a CSV. A city profile (parameter,value rows, or a header row plus one data row) is matched to model settings by name (population, beds per 1000, kilns, average temperature, rainy days, fog, smokers, flights, speed limit and others), limited to each setting's range, and shown in a table before anything changes. A population above 1,000 is modelled as a 500-person slice with the real-world scale set to match. Unknown columns are ignored and listed. An observed series (day or date plus aqi, temp_c, infectious_pct or power_kw) is compared with the model's daily averages (mean absolute error, bias, correlation). Auto-calibrate sets the base temperature from the observed mean and searches kiln count and smoke levels with short background runs to reduce the AQI error.

                    What this is not. Matching a few averages does not make the model a validated copy of your city: the people, roads, buildings and behaviour are still the model's own synthetic ones, and calibration on 3 to 5 short runs is a starting point. Real data you upload is used only in your browser. The sample files are invented numbers for a demonstration.

                    18. The v15 mayor: watching every minute, alternatives, deny list, your ideas, Paper Lab

                    What changed and why. v14's agent decided every few hours from simple thresholds, so it often repeated the same suggestion. v15 senses six pressures every simulated minute, smooths them and tracks their trend, and plans at once when an alarm fires (air-quality forecast passes 200 or 300, infections pass 1, 5 or 15%, hospital 80% or full, a death, an outage, demand near the grid limit, low water, storm, snow, fog, three accidents in two hours). For each problem it finds the likely cause (estimated shares of smoke from kilns, construction dust, factories and traffic; whether fog or low wind traps the air; how many people each case infects) and ranks several different options from a toolbox of about 50 controls, scoring each as expected benefit minus the burden it puts on people. Options already in force overlap with new ones, so each new option is credited with less. Options it has tried before are trusted more or less according to what happened, and rarely tried ones get a small exploration bonus, so it moves on to other options when one does not work.

                    Human in the loop. Proposals show the best option and up to three alternatives. Deny puts an option on a deny list for 1 to 5 simulated days (default 2). It is offered again after that time, or immediately as a new request only if its problem becomes an emergency; it is never applied without your approval in that case. A switch lets the agent act at once in an emergency, but never on something you denied. Early warnings estimate when beds, air quality, power or water will cross a limit if the trend continues.

                    Your own idea. Type what you would do in plain words (English or Roman Urdu keywords). Keyword rules turn it into city settings, then the agent checks it: is the problem area under pressure now, how much burden does it add, which serious problems does it leave untouched, which complementary measures are missing. "Test it" runs fresh copies of the city (3 seeds) with the current settings, with your idea, and with your idea plus the agent's additions, and reports harm, burden and total cost. It only understands the topics listed in the app; anything else is reported as not understood rather than guessed.

                    Paper Lab. Runs a repeatable benchmark (scenarios × controllers × paired seeds), computes mean ± SD, paired differences with 95% confidence intervals, effect sizes and Holm-adjusted p-values, a sensitivity analysis for the burden weight, and writes a paper draft from the numbers. The in-app run is small by default; bench/bench.js runs the same code faster for the full study. Seed repeatability: v15 fixed a bug in which bus-stop queues were shared between simulations, so the same seed and settings now always give identical results, even when two simulations run side by side.

                    Honest limits. Still no language model and no real-city validation. The expected effects and burdens of each option, and the weight of burden in total cost (0.4), are my own judgements, not estimates from data; the benchmark varies only the burden weight. The agent and the scenarios were designed by the same author, and the agent was adjusted using a pilot on other seeds (101 to 103) before the final run on seeds 201 onward. A generated paper is a draft: it cannot make the study valid, and every claim and reference must be checked by a person.

                    19. v16: a team of agents, strict consent, a forecaster, and red-team testing

                    Strict consent. Nothing in the city changes unless you press Approve. The "autonomous" mode and the "act at once in an emergency" switch of v15 were removed from the app; they exist only inside headless experiment copies, to measure what consent costs. Big decisions, standing rules (rules the city then applies by itself, such as automatic kiln inspections), hard-to-undo steps and anything the safety reviewer flags need a second, deliberate click on Confirm (a double click never confirms). Lifting a measure is also a proposal you decide on; any measure can be undone, and an undo never overwrites a value you changed since. Deny keeps an option away for the days you chose; it returns earlier only as a new question if its area becomes an emergency (at most every 6 hours). "Never" keeps it away completely until you allow it. Your deny and never lists survive a restart of the simulation.

                    The team. Four specialist officers (health, air quality, traffic, utilities) watch their areas every minute, explain causes and rank options. A coordinator merges their plans. A safety reviewer annotates every option: combined burden on people, overriding something you set yourself in the last day, earlier denials of similar measures, invalid sensor readings, re-raised denials, standing rules and forecasts. A forecaster copies the current city exactly (people, cars, air, grid and the random-number state) once per option plus once for "do nothing", runs the copies forward 3 to 24 hours with the same random future, and reports what each option did; if none beat doing nothing it says "consider waiting". An auditor writes every decision to a ledger and checks every simulated hour that each change to the city has a recorded reason.

                    Red-team testing. I wrote attacks against the agent core (Node.js) and against the web page (a real browser via Playwright), ran them, fixed what failed, then wrote new attacks against the fixes. The v15 agent resisted 8 of 25 core attacks and 4 of 13 browser attacks: it acted without a click by default, could apply a different option than the one clicked if the list changed, could be blinded for the rest of a run by one invalid sensor value, read "do not close schools" as "close schools", froze on a 3 MB CSV, and a double click could land on the next proposal. Later rounds found that the world-copy routine failed on the page's event listeners (the agent paused itself safely, as designed), that safety switches could be flipped from the browser console, and that a proposal blocked by your role vanished. All of these are fixed and re-tested; the final suite and its results are in bench/. Passing these tests shows that these attacks fail, not that no other attack exists.

                    Tests actually run. 34 of 34 unit tests, 15 of 15 new red-team attacks (overdrawing the treasury, forged exit consent, flapping, extreme settings, hand-edited price tags), 41 of 41 older attacks.                     How the model works

                    A living city of 500 people. Nobody programmed what happens.

                    Every person in Outbreak Lab lives with their family, goes to school or work, the gym, the bazaar, the NADRA office, Friday prayers and weddings. They walk on footpaths or drive through traffic lights, breathe the smoke from brick kilns and fly abroad from the airport. Rain, fog and smog come and go, and accidents happen. The epidemic curve, the smog, the traffic jams, the motorway pile-ups and the full hospital all emerge from people living their daily lives.

                    What is agent-based modelling?

                    An agent-based model (ABM) simulates a system from the bottom up. Instead of one equation for the whole population, we create many individual agents. Each agent has its own properties, follows its own rules and interacts with the agents and places near it. We then run time forward and watch what the whole system does.

                    The classic SEIR equation model treats the population as four big buckets in which everyone meets everyone equally. An ABM can include what the equations leave out: families, schools and workplaces, daily routines, traffic, weather, air pollution, lifestyle, a hospital with a limited number of beds, and plain chance.

                    Agents

                    500 people in about 150 families. Each has an age, a home, a job, a daily plan, a health state, a lifestyle, a stress level, the air they have breathed and a memory of who infected them.

                    Environment

                    A 1 × 0.6 km city of 60 blocks, plus an airport, the M-2 motorway, brick kilns, farmland, weather and an air pollution map.

                    Rules

                    Routines, walking and driving, traffic lights, accidents, contacts in shared places, illness, ambulances, a helicopter, flights, smoke and wind.

                    Time

                    A scheduler runs every rule once per tick. 1 tick = 1 minute, so 1,440 ticks make a day. Each run starts on Monday at 06:00.

                    What happens in every tick

                    The World.step() method runs these rules once per simulated minute:

                    1. New day (at midnight): weather for the day, flights, weddings, travellers leaving and coming home, and every agent's plan.
                    2. Environment: rain and fog levels, darkness, driving speed, traffic lights.
                    3. Act: agents walk or drive to their next activity; cars stop at red lights. Inside a place, agents move around or use a machine, a chair or a prayer row.
                    4. Roads: collisions at junctions, skids, people hit while crossing, the motorway with its car-following, crashes and pile-ups.
                    5. Rescue: ambulances and the helicopter collect injured and seriously ill people.
                    6. Airport: flights land and leave; travellers come home, some with the infection.
                    7. Contacts (every 5 minutes): every infectious person may infect susceptible people in the same place within the contact distance. You can only catch it at home from your own family. People in cars, abroad or in isolation infect nobody.
                    8. Health: incubation and illness timers; recovery or death; detection and isolation.
                    9. Air (every 10 minutes): smoke, wind and mixing; injuries heal; travellers return.
                    10. Hourly: wind changes, stress and air exposure update, statistics and the event feed.

                    The infection rule

                    P(infect per minute of contact) = β/60 × place risk × (0.5 if the sick person wears a mask) × (0.7 if the healthy person wears a mask) × (1 + stress effect × stress) × (1.2 if a smoker)

                    β is the transmission chance per hour of close contact (default 5%). Masks only count outside homes. A vaccinated person is fully protected with a chance equal to the vaccine efficacy.

                    Serious illness and death

                    Seriously ill people fall ill 1.5 to 3.5 days into their illness and stay ill 3 to 6 days longer. Only seriously ill people can die of the disease.

                    P(serious) = age (child 1%, adult 7%, elder 30% of those with symptoms) × 1.8 if a smoker × 0.6 if a gym-goer × (1 + harm from bad air × air breathed) × 0.3 if vaccinated
                    P(death) = death risk (35%) × age factor (child 0.5, adult 0.8, elder 1.4) × (1 + 1.5 × share of 1.5 days spent waiting without a bed) × 0.5 if vaccinated

                    “Air breathed” is 1.0 for someone who has breathed PM2.5 of 150 µg/m³ for days. When all hospital beds are taken by sick and injured people, new patients have to wait at home; after 1.5 days without care their risk of dying is 2.5 times higher.

                    Road accidents

                    risk = driving danger × (speed limit / 50)² × (1 + 1.4 rain + 2.6 fog) × 1.6 at night

                    Per minute: two cars from crossing roads in the same junction collide with chance 0.8% × risk (× 0.4 with a traffic officer); any car crashes alone with chance 0.0011% × risk × (1 + stress); a person crossing within 7 m of a car is hit with chance 0.05% × risk. A driver is injured with chance 35% × speed/50 and killed with chance 3% × (speed/50)²; a seat belt roughly halves both. A person on foot is injured with chance 70% and killed with chance 9% × (speed/50)².

                    Air quality

                    new smoke = old smoke moved by the wind + mixing with neighbours + kilns + chimneys + exhaust − cleaning (2–5% every 10 minutes; ×0.15 in fog, ×0.3 in smog, +12% in rain)

                    The air quality index (AQI) uses the US EPA scale for PM2.5: 0–50 good, 51–100 moderate, 101–150 unhealthy for sensitive groups, 151–200 unhealthy, 201–300 very unhealthy, above 300 hazardous.

                    Emergent behaviour to look for

                    • The daily rhythm. Streets fill at 07:00 and 17:00, queues form at red lights, schools empty at weekends and everyone is home at night.
                    • Homes amplify, schools and workplaces connect. Most infections happen inside homes, but schools, offices, the bazaar and gatherings carry the disease between families.
                    • Smog builds up. On calm, foggy days the kiln smoke cannot escape and the AQI climbs past 200; rain washes it away within hours.
                    • Foggy mornings are dangerous. Accidents cluster in morning fog; if the motorway stays open, one crash becomes a pile-up.
                    • The hospital is shared. Accident victims and motorway casualties take beds that seriously ill patients need.
                    • Super-spreading events. A Friday prayer or a wedding with one infectious guest can start many new chains at once.
                    • Imported cases. Travellers can restart an outbreak that was dying out.
                    • Chance matters. Change only the random seed and the same settings give a different story.

                    Assumptions and limits

                    This is a teaching model of a generic airborne disease in a stylised Pakistani city, not a forecast. People and vehicles move more slowly than in real life so you can watch them; traffic-light cycles, wind and flight times are scaled to match. Accident rates, pollution levels and health effects are chosen to make patterns visible, not calibrated to real data. Real ABMs are validated against real data before they guide decisions.

                    Agent-based modelling in the real world

                    • Public health. Individual-based simulations such as Imperial College London’s CovidSim informed COVID-19 policy in 2020.
                    • Traffic and transport. MATSim and SUMO simulate every traveller and vehicle to plan roads, traffic lights and public transport.
                    • Air quality. Exposure models combine pollution maps with people's daily movements, which is how studies estimate the health cost of smog.
                    • Crowd safety. Evacuation models test how people leave stadiums, stations and large gatherings.
                    • Economics and ecology. Market models with many traders; predator–prey and fishery models.

                    Popular tools for building ABMs include NetLogo, Mesa (Python), Repast, GAMA and AnyLogic.

                    Key words

                    Agent: an independent individual with its own state and rules.

                    Environment: the space where agents live and interact, including weather and air.

                    Emergence: a pattern of the whole system that no single rule describes.

                    Stochastic: includes randomness, so runs differ.

                    Tick: one step of simulated time (here, one minute).

                    AQI: the air quality index, from 0 (clean) to 500 (hazardous).

                    Imported case: an infection caught outside the city and brought in.

                    Car-following: each driver adjusts their speed to the vehicle ahead.
