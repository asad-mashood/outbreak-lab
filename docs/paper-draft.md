## Abstract

Decision-support agents for cities are usually judged by how well they act on their own. We study the opposite design: a team of rule-based agents that may change nothing in a simulated city without a person's explicit approval. Four specialist officers (health, air quality, traffic, utilities) sense six pressure indicators every simulated minute and rank several alternative interventions by expected benefit minus burden on people; a coordinator merges their proposals; a safety reviewer annotates every option (combined burden, conflicts with the person's own recent choices, earlier denials, sensor faults, standing rules); a forecaster runs each top option on an exact copy of the current city with the same random future; and an auditor records every decision and checks hourly that every change has a recorded reason. Denied options go on a deny list for a chosen number of days and return earlier only as a new question in an emergency. In an agent-based city of 500 residents (one-minute step, 4 stress scenarios, 5 paired seeds, 3 simulated days, 140 runs), with total cost defined as harm plus 0.4 × burden, the agent team with a reviewer who approves every proposal after 1 h changed total cost against no intervention by 0.4 points (95% CI -0.7 to 1.5; no reliable difference), lowering harm by 35% while adding 11.2 points of burden. Requiring consent for every action, compared with letting the same agent act alone, gave no reliable difference (0.5 points, 95% CI -0.6 to 1.5). All results describe a synthetic city that has not been validated against real data. Code, seeds, raw results and tests are provided.

**Keywords:** agentic AI; human-in-the-loop; meaningful human control; multi-agent systems; agent-based modelling; decision support; red-teaming; simulation-based forecasting; smart city

## 1. Introduction

Agent-based models (ABMs) are widely used to explore how interventions change outbreaks of disease, and several large ones were built and used during the COVID-19 pandemic [1] [2] [3]. A city government, however, does not choose a package once: it watches a stream of signals, decides what to do and when, hears objections, and revises. It also faces several problems at once. In South Asian cities, for example, winter smog from brick kilns, traffic, factories and construction dust overlaps with infectious disease, fog-related road accidents and a stressed power grid, and satellite remote sensing is being developed to find the kilns [4].

Software agents could help with such decisions, but a city is a setting where an agent acting on its own is hard to justify: restrictions fall on people, mistakes are costly, and responsibility must stay with an accountable person. Classic work on automation distinguishes levels of autonomy from "the computer offers a set of alternatives" to "the computer acts autonomously" [5]; guidelines for human-AI interaction ask systems to make clear what they can do, support efficient dismissal and correction, and explain their behaviour [6]. We therefore build and test an agent team that sits at the "suggest alternatives and execute only if the human approves" level, and ask what that costs and what keeps it safe.

We test five questions with a documented, repeatable protocol:

1. **With a person approving every action, does the agent team pay off against doing nothing and against simple baselines, once the burden of restrictions on people is counted?**
2. **What does requiring consent cost, compared with letting the same agent act on its own?**
3. **Does a forecaster that tries each option on a copy of the current city help the person choose?**
4. **What happens when the person denies half of the proposals?**
5. **Can the system be misused, broken or made to act without permission?** (a red-team evaluation)

Our contributions are: (i) an open, explainable multi-agent design with strict consent (approve, confirm, deny for N days, never, emergency re-entry as a question only, undo, consented lifting of measures) and an auditor; (ii) a forecaster based on exact copies of the running simulation with common random numbers; (iii) a paired-seed benchmark with effect sizes, a burden-weight sensitivity analysis and an A/B test between agent versions; (iv) a red-team test suite run in rounds, with the flaws found and fixed; and (v) an honest account of what the evidence does and does not show.

## 2. Background and related work

**Agent-based models of interventions.** Covasim [2] and OpenABM-Covid19 [3] are open ABMs used to compare non-pharmaceutical interventions such as testing, isolation and contact tracing, alongside earlier modelling of closures and distancing by the Imperial College response team [1]. They fix a set of interventions and study the epidemic; they do not model a decision-maker who must also manage air quality, roads and utilities.

**Learning controllers for epidemic control.** Reinforcement learning has been applied to choose interventions over time, for example to learn prevention strategies for pandemic influenza in a meta-population model of Great Britain's local areas, using proximal policy optimisation [7]. Such controllers can be strong but are hard to inspect and act on their own. We take the opposite trade-off: hand-designed, rule-based agents whose reasoning is readable, with lightweight learning only in how much they trust each action, and no action without consent.

**Agents, autonomy and oversight.** We use "agent" in the sense of an autonomous system that perceives its environment, acts and pursues goals [8]. Levels of automation range from full human control to full computer control, and the right level can differ for information gathering, analysis, decision and action [5]. Our agents automate sensing, analysis and the ranking of options, but leave the decision and its execution to a person. Design guidelines for human-AI interaction [6] motivate several features: alternatives instead of a single answer, explanations, easy dismissal (deny, never), correction (undo) and remembering recent interactions (the deny list).

**Red-teaming.** Red-teaming means deliberately trying to make a system fail or behave harmfully so that the failures can be fixed; it is now common practice for language models [9]. We apply the same idea to a much simpler rule-based system: a growing suite of attacks and misuse cases, run in rounds, with every failure recorded.

**Model description and statistics.** We describe the simulator following the structure of the ODD protocol [10]. Because simulation replicates are cheap, p-values can be made arbitrarily small, so we report effect sizes with confidence intervals and treat p-values only as a screening aid [11].

## 3. The simulated city

The simulator is a single-page JavaScript agent-based model (Outbreak Lab). The description below follows the ODD elements [10] in brief; the source code is the full specification.

**Purpose.** To explore how a city of residents with families, jobs and routines responds to infection, weather, air pollution, traffic accidents and infrastructure stress under different interventions.

**Entities and scales.** 500 resident agents (a representative slice that can be scaled up for resource needs) with age group, household, workplace or school, health state (susceptible, exposed, infectious, recovered, dead from disease, dead from the road), smoking status, chronic conditions and stress. Places include homes, schools, a university, market, mosque, hospital, airport and a motorway. Infrastructure objects include traffic junctions, a power grid with solar and battery, a water tank and pumps, Wi-Fi access points, brick kilns with simulated satellite passes, factories and a construction site. One time step is one simulated minute; a run starts at 06:00 on a Monday.

**Process overview.** Each step moves agents by their schedule and mode of travel, spreads infection through close contacts within a radius with chance depending on masks, distancing, vaccination, stress and air exposure, and updates hospital admissions, ambulances and road accidents. Every ten simulated minutes a pollution grid receives emissions from kilns, factories, vehicles, construction dust and waste burning, is advected by wind and mixed, and is reduced by rain and fog effects. Hourly, the power, water, weather, forecast and common-illness sub-models update.

**Stochasticity, seeds and copies.** All random draws come from seeded generators, so a run is repeatable; in every comparison below, all controllers face the same city for a given seed ("paired seeds"). The whole simulation state, including the random-number state, can be copied exactly; a copy run forward reproduces the original bit for bit and never touches it (verified by unit and red-team tests). The forecaster uses such copies.

**Validation status.** The model has been checked for internal consistency and plausible behaviour, and a small calibration helper can fit temperature and air-quality levels to uploaded data, but it has **not** been validated against a real city. All results should be read as statements about the simulator.

## 4. The agent team

**Specialist officers (sensing and diagnosis).** Every simulated minute four officers read six pressure indicators between 0 and 1: health (infected share, hospital load, deaths), air quality (the larger of the current air-quality index and the forecast peak), road safety (recent accidents and weather hazard), power, water and connectivity. Each indicator is smoothed (time constant about ten minutes) and given a trend per hour; invalid readings are ignored and reported. Goal weights combine them into one **harm** score J. A **burden** score B in [0, about 1] sums the cost of restrictions on people that are in force (closing schools 0.25, markets 0.20, 40% work from home 0.14, and so on; Appendix A). Total cost is **C = J + 0.4 × B**. Each officer explains the cause of its problem in words (for example the estimated source shares of fine particles, or how many people each case infects).

**Coordinator (planning with alternatives).** Edge-triggered alarms (air quality passes 200 or 300; infections pass 1%, 5%, 15%; hospital 80% or full; a new death; a grid outage; demand near capacity; low water tank; storm, snow, thick fog; three accidents in two hours; repeated sensor faults) wake the planner immediately; otherwise it plans at a fixed interval. For each area with non-zero urgency it scores every available option a as  score(a) = U × e(a) × L(a) × X(a) × w − μ × b(a) × (1 − 0.4 U), with μ = 0.4, where U is the urgency (the larger of the smoothed pressure and a projection of its trend three hours ahead, twelve for health), e(a) is a context-dependent prior of how much of the problem the option can remove (reduced when other measures already cover the same problem), L(a) is a learned trust factor (0.4 to 1.6), X(a) a small exploration bonus, w the area's share of the goal weight and b(a) the burden of the option. Options already in force, on cool-down, on the deny list or on the never list are removed; the best option and up to three alternatives of different kinds are proposed. Urgency is classed as low, normal, high or emergency.

**Safety reviewer.** Before an option is shown, a reviewer attaches notes and raises a caution when: the combined burden after approval would exceed 35/100; the option would override a setting the person changed in the last day; the person denied a similar measure; sensor readings were invalid in the last hour; the option is a re-raised denial; or the forecaster saw it do worse. It also marks big decisions, standing rules (rules the city then applies by itself) and hard-to-undo steps. Any of these requires a second, deliberate confirmation.

**Forecaster.** For the top options of each proposal the forecaster copies the current city once per option plus once for "do nothing", applies the option to its copy, and runs all copies forward for a horizon that depends on the area (24 h for health, 12 h for air, roads and water, 6 h for power, 3 h for connectivity). All copies share the same random future (common random numbers), so differences come from the option, not from chance. It reports the change in harm, burden and total cost, re-ranks the options only if the forecast clearly reverses the order (by more than one point), and says "consider waiting" if no option did better than doing nothing. One copy is one possible future of the model, not a probability.

**Consent protocol.** In the live tool nothing changes without the person: Approve (and Confirm where required) executes exactly the option and version that was shown; a click on an outdated version is refused. Deny puts the option on a deny list for 1 to 5 days (default 2); it returns earlier only if its area becomes an emergency, and then only as a new question, at most every six hours. Never keeps it away until the person allows it. Measures are never lifted automatically: the agents propose lifting (also reviewed), and the person can undo any measure; an undo or lift never overwrites a value the person changed since. The safety switches are fixed when the agent is created and cannot be flipped later. If an agent fails internally it pauses itself without changing the city.

**Auditor and learning.** Every decision (approved, denied, never, allowed, undone, rejected, blocked) is written to a ledger. Every simulated hour the auditor compares the city's settings with the previous hour and flags any change without a recorded reason. Twelve hours after an action, the agents compare the target pressure with a baseline that extends the pre-action trend, label the action "helped", "no clear effect" or "hurt", and update L(a); this verdict is a heuristic, not a causal estimate. A free-text idea from the person is read with keyword rules (English and Roman Urdu, with negation handling: "do not close schools" is never read as closing them), critiqued, and can be tried on copies of the current city.

**What these agents are not.** They contain no language model and learn only the trust factors. The priors e(a) and burdens b(a) were set by the authors and have not been estimated from data.

## 5. Experimental design

**Scenarios.** 4 stress scenarios, each a set of changes to the default city (Table 1).

| Scenario | Description |
| --- | --- |
| Epidemic | More first cases, easier spread, fewer hospital beds |
| Winter smog | Winter fog, 8 kilns burning all winter, heavy factory smoke |
| Heat, storm and power cuts | Heatwave, a storm, a weak grid with frequent outages, high water use |
| Compound winter crisis | Epidemic, smog and power cuts together in winter |


*Table 1. Scenarios.*

**Controllers.** 7 controllers (Table 2). The person is simulated: "approves after 1 h" approves the top option (confirming where needed) once a proposal has waited 60 simulated minutes, including proposals to lift measures; the forecast-following reviewer waits for the forecasts, approves the option with the best forecast net effect if it is positive and otherwise denies all options of that proposal; the "denies half" reviewer answers after 120 minutes and denies with probability 0.5. The "acting alone" variants exist only in simulation to measure the cost of consent; the tool itself cannot run them. All variants use the balanced goal and start with no learned trust.

| Code | Controller |
| --- | --- |
| none | No intervention |
| static | Fixed precaution pack (always on) |
| autopilot | City's built-in autopilot (smog alerts) |
| mayor1 | Mayor acting alone: alarms + plans every 30 min |
| mayor360 | Mayor acting alone: plans every 6 h, no alarms |
| consent | Mayor with consent: a reviewer approves every action after 1 h |
| hitl | Mayor with consent: reviewer denies half |


*Table 2. Controllers.*

**Design.** 5 seeds (201 to 205) × 4 scenarios × 7 controllers = 140 runs of 3 simulated days. For each scenario and seed all controllers see the same city. The v15 agent parameters were tuned on pilot seeds 101 to 103. While designing the forecast-following reviewer we also looked at single runs on seed 201, which is part of the evaluation set; no numeric parameter was tuned on it, but the design choice was made after seeing it.

**Outcomes.** Harm J and burden B are averaged over the run (sampled every ten minutes); total cost C = J + 0.4 B (all shown as points on a 0-100 scale). Secondary outcomes: peak share infected, peak air-quality index, deaths, accidents, minutes of power outage, and agent activity.

**Analysis.** For each comparison we report the mean paired difference (baseline minus controller, so positive means the controller is better), its 95% confidence interval from a paired t distribution, the standardised paired effect d_z and the share of seeds in which the controller was better. Tests against no intervention are Holm-adjusted; pooled tests use all 20 scenario-seed pairs. Conclusions rest on effect sizes and intervals rather than p-values [11].

## 7. Discussion

**Does the consent-based team pay off?** The agent team with a reviewer who approves every proposal after 1 h against no intervention showed no reliable difference (0.4 points, 95% CI -0.7 to 1.5). It lowered harm by 4.9 points and added 11.2 points of burden. In 1 of 4 scenarios the interval lay above zero (Heat, storm and power cuts); it was reliably worse in Epidemic.

**The cost of consent.** Requiring approval for every action against letting the agent act alone gave no reliable difference (0.5 points, 95% CI -0.6 to 1.5): harm rose by 0.6 points and burden fell by 2.6 points. The simulated person here approves everything after an hour, so this isolates the effect of the delay and of lifting only with consent, not the effect of human judgement.

**A reviewer who denies half.** Against a reviewer who approves everything this gave no reliable difference (0.6 points, 95% CI -0.4 to 1.6). The deny list and its emergency re-entry rule kept running: on average 1.8 re-raised questions and 7.9 denials per run. Because denying also means acting less, the benchmark cannot separate the value of the deny list from the value of restraint.

**The burden weight decides the verdict.** The pattern across Tables 4 and 8 is that the agents reliably lower harm but buy that with restrictions on people. The agent team with a reviewer who approves every proposal after 1 h pays off only while μ is below about 0.43 (we used 0.4). The right weight is a value judgement for each city, not a measurement; the tool lets the person see both parts separately for this reason.

## 8. Limitations

1. **Synthetic city, no real-world validation.** The model has not been calibrated or validated against a real city. Results describe the simulator.
2. **Authored priors.** The expected effects e(a), burdens b(a) and the weight 0.4 of burden in the total cost are the authors' judgements. We varied only the burden weight (Section 6.5), not the individual priors.
3. **Same designers wrote the scenarios and the agents.** Favourable results may reflect fit to these scenarios. Parameters were tuned on pilot seeds 101 to 103; the reported results use different seeds, but the design choices were still shaped by this model.
4. **Simulated people.** All reviewers are simple scripts. Real people would read the explanations, sometimes disagree with the forecaster, get tired of questions and make mistakes; none of that is measured. A user study is needed.
5. **One possible future.** The forecaster runs one copy per option with the same random future; it shows a plausible outcome under the model, not a probability, and it inherits all of the model's errors. Its horizon (24 h for health) is shorter than the time most health measures need to work, so it mostly sees their cost and tends to advise against them; a reviewer who follows it therefore leans towards lower burden.
6. **Short horizon and small counts.** Runs last 3 simulated days with 500 residents, so deaths are rare and noisy; the cost measure relies mostly on composite pressure indicators.
7. **Red-team scope.** The attacks were written by the developers, who also wrote the defences. The page runs locally in one browser; someone with full control of that browser can still change its code. The suite tests the logic and the interface, not a networked deployment.
8. **Statistics.** Replicates are independent across seeds but not across scenarios sharing seed numbers; pooled intervals are indicative.

## 9. Conclusion

Inside a synthetic city, a team of explainable agents that may change nothing without a person's approval did not reliably change total cost against doing nothing (0.4 points, 3%) at a burden weight of 0.4. With a simulated person who approves within an hour, consent was not measurably costly compared with letting the agent act alone. A red-team suite run in rounds found and removed real flaws. The more useful output may be the protocol: a transparent agent team, strict consent, paired-seed experiments, an A/B test and a red-team suite that others can rerun and extend. The next steps are a user study with real decision-makers and validation against a real city's data.
